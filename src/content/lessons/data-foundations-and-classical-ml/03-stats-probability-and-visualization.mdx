Numbers in a table rarely tell the whole story on their own. Two datasets can share the exact same mean and standard deviation while looking completely different when plotted. That is why statistics and visualization always work together.

Here's an overview of how I approach any unfamiliar dataset: first I quantify center, spread, and correlation; then I plot distributions and relationships to verify what the numbers claim.

## Concept Walkthrough: Distributions, Outliers & Relationships

### Quartiles and the IQR Rule

When data is skewed, the **median** and **quartiles** tell a much cleaner story than the mean alone. The Interquartile Range measures the middle 50% of your data:

$$\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$$

Values falling outside $[Q_1 - 1.5 \cdot \text{IQR},\; Q_3 + 1.5 \cdot \text{IQR}]$ are standard boxplot outlier candidates.

### Measuring Linear Relationships

To quantify how strongly two numeric features $x$ and $y$ move together, we compute the **Pearson correlation coefficient** $r \in [-1, 1]$:

$$r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \bar{y})^2}}$$

Values near $+1$ or $-1$ indicate a strong linear trend; values near $0$ mean little linear association (though a non-linear curve could still exist).

### Choosing the Right Chart

| Analytical Question | Best Chart | Matplotlib Call |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Compare quantities across categories | Bar chart | `ax.bar(categories, values)` |
| Inspect shape and skew of one variable | Histogram | `ax.hist(x, bins=20)` |
| Compare medians, spread, and outliers | Box plot | `ax.boxplot([group_a, group_b])` |
| Spot correlation or clusters in two variables | Scatter plot | `ax.scatter(x, y, alpha=0.7)` |

### 1. Try It in Browser (Quartiles, IQR Outliers & Pearson $r$)

Run this NumPy snippet to compute quartiles, detect outliers with the $1.5 \cdot \text{IQR}$ rule, and measure correlation:

<PythonRunner

  packages={["numpy"]}

weights = np.array([180, 215, 210, 188, 176, 209, 200, 231, 185, 160, 197, 295], dtype=float)
heights = np.array([72, 75, 74, 71, 70, 74, 73, 77, 71, 68, 73, 79], dtype=float)

q1, median, q3 = np.percentile(weights, [25, 50, 75])
iqr = q3 - q1
upper_fence = q3 + 1.5 * iqr
outliers = weights[weights > upper_fence]

corr = np.corrcoef(heights, weights)[0, 1]

print(f"Q1={q1:.1f}, Median={median:.1f}, Q3={q3:.1f}, IQR={iqr:.1f}")
print(f"Upper fence: {upper_fence:.1f} | Outliers: {outliers}")
print(f"Pearson r (height vs weight): {corr:.3f}")`}

### 2. Diagnostic Plots in Google Colab

Open the Colab notebook to generate labeled histograms, multi-group boxplots, and annotated scatter plots using `matplotlib` and `scipy.stats`:

<ColabButton notebook="03-stats-probability-and-visualization.ipynb" />

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
study_hours = rng.uniform(1, 10, size=80)
exam_scores = 45 + 5.2 * study_hours + rng.normal(0, 4, size=80)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4))
axes[0].hist(exam_scores, bins=12, color="#A7452A", edgecolor="white")
axes[0].set_title("Exam Score Distribution")
axes[0].set_xlabel("Score")

axes[1].scatter(study_hours, exam_scores, color="#A7452A", alpha=0.8)
axes[1].set_title("Study Hours vs. Exam Score")
axes[1].set_xlabel("Hours")
axes[1].set_ylabel("Score")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```
