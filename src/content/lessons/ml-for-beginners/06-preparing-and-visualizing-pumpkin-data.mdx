Real-world datasets rarely arrive clean and ready for `model.fit()`. When we inspect the 1,757-row USDA **North American Pumpkin Market** spreadsheet (`US-pumpkins.csv`), we immediately hit blanks, mixed strings, and a tricky `Package` column mixing `'1 1/9 bushel'`, `'1/2 bushel'`, `'bins'`, and `'each'`. Before we can predict pumpkin prices, we have to normalize units and visualize the data properly.

---

## Cleaning & Normalizing Bushel Prices

To answer *"What is the average price of a pumpkin per bushel in a given month?"*, we follow a four-step Pandas cleaning pipeline:

1. **Check Missing Data & Select Columns**: Inspect `pumpkins.isnull().sum()` and slice `['Package', 'Low Price', 'High Price', 'Date']` with `.loc`.
2. **Filter to Consistent Units**: Keep only rows sold by the bushel (`pumpkins['Package'].str.contains('bushel', case=True, regex=True)`), narrowing 1,757 rows down to ~415 comparable records.
3. **Compute Average Price & Month**:
   $$\text{Price} = \frac{\text{Low Price} + \text{High Price}}{2}, \qquad \text{Month} = \text{DatetimeIndex}(\text{Date}).\text{month}$$
4. **Normalize Per-Bushel Pricing**: Divide `1 1/9 bushel` prices by $(1 + \frac{1}{9})$ and `1/2 bushel` prices by $\frac{1}{2}$ so every row sits on an apples-to-apples per-bushel scale.

*Fun observation*: Half-bushel pumpkins still look noticeably pricier per bushel because miniature decorative pumpkins pack far more individual pumpkins—with less hollow air space—into a bushel than large pie pumpkins!

## Visualizing with Matplotlib vs. Seaborn

| Library | Best Used For | Key Functions in This Lesson |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Matplotlib** | Fine-grained control over axes, labels, and grouped aggregations | `plt.scatter(price, month)`, `groupby('Month')['Price'].mean().plot(kind='bar')` |
| **Seaborn** | Expressive statistical charts directly from Pandas DataFrames | `sns.relplot(..., kind="line")`, `sns.catplot(..., kind="bar")`, `sns.heatmap(corr, annot=True)` |

A raw `plt.scatter(price, month)` looks noisy and unhelpful, whereas a grouped bar chart or `sns.catplot(x="Month", y="Price", kind="bar")` immediately reveals the **September–October harvest peak**. Meanwhile, a correlation `heatmap` teaches a crucial lesson: `Month` has weak linear correlation with `Price` even though the bar chart shows a clear autumn peak, because correlation coefficients only measure *straight-line* relationships.

## Try It Yourself: Bushel Normalizer & Seasonal Mean Bar Table

Let's run the exact USDA pumpkin bushel filter, `1 1/9` and `1/2` price normalization, and monthly average aggregation in pure Python.

  initialCode={`from collections import defaultdict

raw_rows = [
    {"Date": "08/24/2016", "Package": "1 1/9 bushel cartons", "Low": 18.0, "High": 20.0},
    {"Date": "08/31/2016", "Package": "1/2 bushel cartons",   "Low": 12.0, "High": 13.0},
    {"Date": "09/17/2016", "Package": "1 1/9 bushel cartons", "Low": 21.0, "High": 23.0},
    {"Date": "09/24/2016", "Package": "1/2 bushel cartons",   "Low": 14.0, "High": 15.0},
    {"Date": "10/08/2016", "Package": "1 1/9 bushel cartons", "Low": 25.0, "High": 30.0},
    {"Date": "10/15/2016", "Package": "24 inch bins",         "Low": 120.0, "High": 140.0},
    {"Date": "11/05/2016", "Package": "1 1/9 bushel crates",  "Low": 21.0, "High": 22.333},
]

bushel_rows = [r for r in raw_rows if "bushel" in r["Package"]]
print(f"Filtered bushel rows: {len(bushel_rows)} of {len(raw_rows)}")

monthly_prices = defaultdict(list)
for r in bushel_rows:
    month = int(r["Date"].split("/")[0])
    avg_price = (r["Low"] + r["High"]) / 2.0
    if "1 1/9" in r["Package"]:
        norm_price = avg_price / (1.0 + 1.0 / 9.0)
    elif "1/2" in r["Package"]:
        norm_price = avg_price / 0.5
    else:
        norm_price = avg_price
    monthly_prices[month].append(norm_price)

print("Mean Price by Month:")
for m in sorted(monthly_prices):
    vals = monthly_prices[m]
    print(f"  Month {m:2d}: $" f"{sum(vals)/len(vals):.2f}")`}
