## Probability & Random Variables

**Probability** is a number in $[0, 1]$ measuring how likely an event is—defined as favorable outcomes divided by total equally likely outcomes. A **random variable** $X$ maps outcomes from a **sample space** $S$ to numbers:

- **Discrete Random Variable**: Takes countable values (e.g., rolling a die, $P(X=3) = \frac{1}{6}$). Its **uniform distribution** assigns $\frac{1}{N}$ to each of $N$ elements.
- **Continuous Random Variable**: Takes values across a real interval $[a, b]$ (e.g., bus arrival time). Because there are infinitely many exact instants, the probability at any exact point $t$ is $0$! Instead, we integrate a **probability density function** $p(x)$ over an interval:

$$P(t_1 \le X < t_2) = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} p(x)\,dx$$

## Mean, Variance, Median & Box-Plot Outliers

Given $n$ samples $x_1, \dots, x_n$, the **mean** $\mu$ (and distribution expectation $\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum x_i p_i$) measures the center, while **variance** $\sigma^2$ and **standard deviation** $\sigma$ measure spread:

$$\mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \mu)^2$$

Because extreme values pull the mean, we also use the **median** (50th percentile), **mode** (most frequent category—helpful when distributions are *multimodal*), and **quartiles**:
- **$Q_1$**: 25% of observations fall below this value.
- **$Q_3$**: 75% of observations fall below this value.
- **Inter-Quartile Range ($\text{IQR}$)**: $\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$. Values outside $[Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR},\, Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}]$ are flagged as **outliers** on a box plot.

## Normal Distribution, Confidence Intervals & Hypothesis Testing

Many real-world measurements—like baseball player weights—cluster around a mean in a bell-shaped **normal distribution** $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. Thanks to the **Central Limit Theorem**, the mean of sufficiently large independent samples from *any* distribution approaches a normal distribution, and by the **Law of Large Numbers**, the sample mean converges to the true population mean $\mu$ as $n \to \infty$.

When we only observe a sample of size $n$ with standard deviation $s$, we estimate the population mean $\mu$ using a **confidence interval** built from **Student's $t$-distribution** (published by William Sealy Gosset under the pseudonym "Student" while working at the Guinness brewery):

$$\bar{X} \pm t_{(1-p)/2,\; n-1} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

Higher confidence ($p = 0.95$ vs $0.85$) produces a wider interval:

| Confidence Level ($p$) | Weight Mean ($\text{lbs}$) | Interval Width |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `0.85` | $201.73 \pm 0.94$ | Narrowest |
| `0.90` | $201.73 \pm 1.08$ | Moderate |
| `0.95` | $201.73 \pm 1.28$ | Widest |

In the MLB dataset, First Basemen average $74.00\text{ in}$ tall while Second Basemen average $71.36\text{ in}$. To prove this difference isn't sampling noise, we run **Student's / Welch's $t$-test** (`scipy.stats.ttest_ind`), checking whether the resulting $p$-value is tiny (e.g., $p < 0.01$).

---

## Covariance & Correlation

To measure whether two variables $X$ and $Y$ move together, we compute **covariance** and normalize it by their standard deviations to get the **Pearson correlation coefficient** $r_{XY} \in [-1, 1]$:

$$\text{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])\big], \qquad r_{XY} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}$$

Remember: correlation ($r \approx 0.53$ between MLB height and weight) shows mathematical association, *not* causation.
