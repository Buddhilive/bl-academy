## Probability & Random Variables

**Probability** is simply a score between $0$ (impossible) and $1$ (guaranteed) that measures how likely an event is—calculated as the number of ways an event can happen divided by the total number of equally likely outcomes. A **random variable** $X$ is just a label for the numeric outcome of a random process (like the number you roll on a die):

- **Discrete Random Variable**: Takes countable, separate values (e.g., rolling a 6-sided die, where $P(X=3) = \frac{1}{6}$). When every outcome is equally likely, it's called a **uniform distribution** (each of $N$ outcomes gets $\frac{1}{N}$).
- **Continuous Random Variable**: Takes any value along a smooth range (e.g., the exact second a bus arrives between 8:00 and 8:15). Because there are infinitely many exact instants, the chance of hitting one *exact* instant to the nanosecond is $0$. Instead, we ask for the chance it arrives within a time window by measuring the area under its probability curve:

$$P(t_1 \le X < t_2) = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} p(x)\,dx$$

Here, $t_1$ and $t_2$ are the start and end of your window (say, 8:05 and 8:10), $p(x)$ is the **probability density function** (the height of the curve at point $x$), and the integral symbol $\int$ simply means "add up the total area under the curve between $t_1$ and $t_2$."

## Mean, Variance, Median & Box-Plot Outliers

Imagine comparing two baseball teams that both average 195 lbs per player. On one team, everyone weighs between 185 and 205 lbs; on the other, half the roster is 160 lbs and half is 230 lbs. To capture both the "typical" value and how spread out the numbers are, we use the **mean** ($\mu$) for the center, and **variance** ($\sigma^2$) or **standard deviation** ($\sigma$) for the spread:

$$\mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \mu)^2$$

In these formulas:
- $n$ is the total number of data points (e.g., players), and $x_i$ is each individual value.
- $\mu$ (pronounced "mu") is the **mean** (add all values together and divide by $n$). For a probability distribution, this average is also called the **expected value** $\mathbb{E}[X]$.
- $\sigma^2$ ("sigma squared") is the **variance**—the average of the squared distances from the mean—and taking its square root gives the **standard deviation** $\sigma$, which brings the spread back into the original units (like pounds).

Because a few extreme values can pull the mean upward or downward, we also rely on the **median** (the exact middle value when sorted), the **mode** (the most common value—useful when data has multiple peaks, or is *multimodal*), and **quartiles**:
- **$Q_1$ (25th percentile)**: 25% of observations fall below this value.
- **$Q_3$ (75th percentile)**: 75% of observations fall below this value.
- **Inter-Quartile Range ($\text{IQR}$)**: $\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$, which measures the span of the middle 50% of the data. Values outside $[Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR},\, Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}]$ are flagged as **outliers** on a box plot.

## Normal Distribution, Confidence Intervals & Hypothesis Testing

Many real-world measurements—like baseball player weights—cluster around an average in a symmetric bell-shaped **normal distribution** $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ (defined by its center $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$). Thanks to the **Central Limit Theorem**, if you take many random samples from *almost any* dataset and average each sample, those sample averages will form a bell curve—and by the **Law of Large Numbers**, the bigger your sample size $n$, the closer your sample average gets to the true population average $\mu$.

Think of a **confidence interval** as a safety net around your sample average. Instead of claiming the entire league's average weight is *exactly* $201.73\text{ lbs}$ based on a sample, you give a range built from **Student's $t$-distribution** (published by William Sealy Gosset under the pseudonym "Student" while working at the Guinness brewery):

$$\bar{X} \pm t_{(1-p)/2,\; n-1} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

Here, $\bar{X}$ is your sample mean, $s$ is your sample standard deviation, $n$ is the sample size, $p$ is your desired confidence level (such as $0.95$ for 95%), and $t_{(1-p)/2,\; n-1}$ is a multiplier from the $t$-distribution table that grows larger when you want higher confidence or have a smaller sample. Higher confidence ($p = 0.95$ vs $0.85$) produces a wider safety net:

| Confidence Level ($p$) | Weight Mean ($\text{lbs}$) | Interval Width |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `0.85` | $201.73 \pm 0.94$ | Narrowest |
| `0.90` | $201.73 \pm 1.08$ | Moderate |
| `0.95` | $201.73 \pm 1.28$ | Widest |

In the MLB dataset, First Basemen average $74.00\text{ in}$ tall while Second Basemen average $71.36\text{ in}$. To check whether this difference is real or just random luck, we run **Student's / Welch's $t$-test** (`scipy.stats.ttest_ind`) and check if the **$p$-value** (the probability of seeing such a gap by pure chance) is tiny (e.g., $p < 0.01$).

## Covariance & Correlation

Do taller baseball players tend to weigh more? To check whether two variables $X$ (height) and $Y$ (weight) move together, we first measure **covariance**—whether above-average values of $X$ pair with above-average values of $Y$—and then scale it into the **Pearson correlation coefficient** $r_{XY}$ so it always sits neatly between $-1$ and $+1$:

$$\text{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])\big], \qquad r_{XY} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}$$

Here, $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\mathbb{E}[Y]$ are the averages of $X$ and $Y$, $\sigma_X$ and $\sigma_Y$ are their standard deviations, and $r_{XY}$ tells you the strength and direction of the linear relationship ($+1$ means a perfect upward line, $0$ means no linear link, and $-1$ means a perfect downward line).

Remember: correlation ($r \approx 0.53$ between MLB height and weight) shows mathematical association, *not* causation.

## Try It Yourself: MLB Descriptive Stats, CI & Correlation

Run this pure-Python statistical engine on the first 20 Major League Baseball player weights and heights to compute mean, standard deviation, IQR outlier bounds, 95% confidence interval, and Pearson correlation.

**MLB Stats, IQR Outliers & Correlation**

```python
import math

weights = [180.0, 215.0, 210.0, 210.0, 188.0, 176.0, 209.0, 200.0, 231.0, 180.0,
           188.0, 180.0, 185.0, 160.0, 180.0, 185.0, 197.0, 189.0, 185.0, 219.0]
heights = [74.0, 74.0, 72.0, 72.0, 73.0, 69.0, 69.0, 71.0, 76.0, 71.0,
           73.0, 73.0, 74.0, 74.0, 69.0, 70.0, 72.0, 73.0, 75.0, 78.0]

n = len(weights)
mean_w = sum(weights) / n
std_w = math.sqrt(sum((w - mean_w) ** 2 for w in weights) / (n - 1))

s = sorted(weights)
q1 = (s[4] + s[5]) / 2
median = (s[9] + s[10]) / 2
q3 = (s[14] + s[15]) / 2
iqr = q3 - q1

# t_0.025, df=19 = 2.093
ci_margin = 2.093 * (std_w / math.sqrt(n))

mean_h = sum(heights) / n
cov = sum((w - mean_w) * (h - mean_h) for w, h in zip(weights, heights)) / (n - 1)
std_h = math.sqrt(sum((h - mean_h) ** 2 for h in heights) / (n - 1))
r = cov / (std_w * std_h)

print(f"Weight Mean: {mean_w:.2f} lbs | Std Dev: {std_w:.2f} lbs")
print(f"Q1: {q1} | Median: {median} | Q3: {q3} | IQR: {iqr}")
print(f"Outlier Bounds: [{q1 - 1.5*iqr:.2f}, {q3 + 1.5*iqr:.2f}]")
print(f"95% Confidence Interval: {mean_w:.2f} ± {ci_margin:.2f} lbs")
print(f"Height-Weight Correlation r: {r:.3f}")
```

**Expected Output:**

```text
Weight Mean: 193.35 lbs | Std Dev: 17.49 lbs
Q1: 180.0 | Median: 188.0 | Q3: 209.5 | IQR: 29.5
Outlier Bounds: [135.75, 253.75]
95% Confidence Interval: 193.35 ± 8.19 lbs
Height-Weight Correlation r: 0.321
```
